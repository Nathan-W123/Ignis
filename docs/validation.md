# Validation

*Validation asks whether the equations describe reality. Whether they are
solved correctly is [`verification.md`](verification.md).*

Ignis is validated against **external, independently produced reference data**,
not against itself. Seven independent sources are used — three other codes,
three experiments, and one engine's published operating data:

| Reference | What it provides | How it was obtained |
|---|---|---|
| **NASA CEA** (Gordon & McBride, NASA RP-1311) | Rocket performance: T<sub>c</sub>, M, γ, c\*, I<sub>sp</sub>, exit states, frozen and shifting | Through `rocketcea`, which wraps the actual NASA Glenn FORTRAN — not a reimplementation |
| **Cantera 3.x** | Species thermodynamics, transport, equilibrium composition | `tools/make_reference_data.py`, using `nasa_gas.yaml` and `gri30.yaml` |
| **Reference equations of state** (Setzmann & Wagner 1991, Leachman et al. 2009, Schmidt & Wagner 1985) | Coolant ρ, c<sub>p</sub>, μ, k | Through CoolProp, tabulated by `tools/build_coolant_tables.py` |
| **JPL TR 32-415** (Back, Massier & Gier 1965) | Measured local gas-side heat flux, heated air through a convergent-divergent nozzle | Transcribed by hand from the scanned report ([§5b](#5b-gas-side-heat-transfer-against-measurements)) |
| **NASA TN D-2832** (Schacht, Quentmeyer & Jones 1965) | Measured gas-side heat-transfer constants, LOX/GH<sub>2</sub> heat-sink rocket | Transcribed by hand from the scanned report ([§5b](#5b-gas-side-heat-transfer-against-measurements)) |
| **NASA TN D-130** (Hatch & Papell 1959) | Measured film-cooled wall temperatures, helium films in heated air | Transcribed by hand from the scanned report ([§5c](#5c-film-cooling-against-its-own-data)) |
| ***SSME Orientation*** (Boeing Rocketdyne 1998) | The RS-25's turbopump operating data at 104.5 % of rated power: flows, pressures, temperatures, efficiencies, horsepower | Transcribed by hand into the test ([§5d](#5d-turbopumps-against-the-rs-25s-published-data)) |

The first three sources are regenerated by `tools/make_reference_data.py`; the
last four are 1959–1998 documents and cannot be. The first six are committed
under `validation/reference/`; the RS-25's numbers are written into
`tests/validation/test_cycle_validation.cpp` with the page they come from.
Every comparison runs as part of the ordinary test suite
(`./scripts/validate.sh`).

Everything below is a **measured** number printed by that script. Nothing here
is asserted without having been run.

The jacket coolant for RP-1 is a **surrogate**: n-dodecane on its reference

RP-1 surrogates (Huber et al., Energy & Fuels 2009) need four components that
CoolProp does not carry.

### 1.5 Why the residual disagreement exists

These are not the same calculation done twice. Ignis uses the **NASA TM-4513
(1993) 7-coefficient** thermodynamic set; CEA 2002 uses the **9-coefficient**
set, fitted separately and over different temperature ranges, and its default
species list for a C/H/O system is slightly larger than the 26 species Ignis
carries for LOX/CH<sub>4</sub>. A one-to-two-part-in-10³ disagreement in flame
temperature is what that difference costs, and it is the dominant term: the
solver-to-solver agreement on *identical* data is six orders of magnitude
tighter (§2.1).

The test tolerances are set at the measured spread with a small margin
(1 % on T, 0.5 % on M and γ, 1 % on c\*, 1.5 % on I<sub>sp</sub> for methane),
so a real regression in the chemistry would fail the suite while the known
database difference does not.

## 2. Chemistry against Cantera

### 2.1 Adiabatic equilibrium on identical data — 65 states

Both Ignis and Cantera are given the **same** NASA-7 coefficients and the same
species list, and asked for the same constant-enthalpy, constant-pressure
equilibrium (LOX/CH<sub>4</sub> and LOX/H<sub>2</sub>, several O/F and
pressures). This isolates the minimiser from the data:

| Quantity | Worst relative disagreement |
|---|---|
| Flame temperature | **1.74e-10** |
| Mean molar mass | **1.40e-10** |
| Frozen c<sub>p</sub> | **3.38e-11** |
| Mole fraction (any species) | **3.36e-09** |

Two independent minimisers — Ignis's descent formulation in
(ln n<sub>j</sub>, ln n, π<sub>i</sub>, ln T) and Cantera's `equilibrate` —
land on the same point to nine or ten digits. That is the strongest statement
available about the equilibrium solver.

### 2.2 Species thermodynamics — 441 species/temperature pairs

Same coefficients, different implementations, over 40 species from 200 K to
6000 K. Worst relative disagreement: c<sub>p</sub> **4.56e-12**, h **4.55e-12**,
s **3.42e-12** — round-off, on a reference file carrying twelve significant
digits.

Getting here required one non-obvious fix. NASA TM-4513 uses a standard-state
pressure of **1 bar**, while Cantera's CHEMKIN-derived input files default to
**1 atm**. Until Cantera's reference pressure was reset to 1 bar in
`tools/make_reference_data.py`, the entropies disagreed by ~5e-4 relative —
exactly R ln(1.01325)/s°, i.e. a units convention, not a bug in either code.
That kind of discrepancy is worth writing down: it is the sort of thing that
gets quietly absorbed into a fudge factor.

### 2.3 Species thermodynamics against an *independent fit* — 305 points

GRI-Mech 3.0 is a separately fitted set for the same species. The spread
between published data sets is a genuine modelling uncertainty, so it is
measured rather than suppressed:

| Comparison | Worst |
|---|---|
| Major species (H<sub>2</sub>O, CO<sub>2</sub>, CO, H<sub>2</sub>, O<sub>2</sub>, OH, O, H, N<sub>2</sub>, CH<sub>4</sub>, NO) | **0.43 %** at CH<sub>4</sub>, 1500 K |
| All species | **10.1 %** at CH<sub>3</sub>O, 500 K |

CH<sub>3</sub>O is a trace radical whose published fits genuinely disagree at
that level; it has no measurable effect on rocket performance. This is
reported, not hidden, because it is the honest size of the data uncertainty.

### 2.4 Transport properties — 36 pure-species points, 12 mixtures

| Comparison | Worst |
|---|---|
| Pure-species viscosity, non-polar | **0.59 %** at H<sub>2</sub>, 3500 K |
| Pure-species viscosity, polar (Brokaw correction) | **3.68 %** at H<sub>2</sub>O, 1000 K |
| Mixture viscosity (Wilke) | **3.07 %** |
| Mixture conductivity | **4.24 %** |

The polar and conductivity gaps are expected and are *model* differences, not
coding errors: Ignis uses the modified Eucken relation for the internal
contribution to k, while Cantera uses the full multicomponent kinetic-theory
expression. The nominal LOX/CH<sub>4</sub> chamber mixture gives
μ = 1.0367e-4 Pa·s against Cantera's 1.03836e-4 (−0.16 %), so the quantity the
Bartz correlation actually consumes agrees far better than the worst case
suggests.

## 3. Coolant properties against reference equations of state

The tabulated coolant properties are compared against CoolProp evaluations of
the same reference EOS at points *between* table nodes, so the comparison
measures the interpolation, not the source:

| Property | Worst interpolation error |
|---|---|
| Density | **1.96 %** |
| Isobaric heat capacity | **0.77 %** |
| Viscosity | **0.99 %** |
| Thermal conductivity | **1.01 %** |

The density figure is dominated by the near-critical region, where ρ(T) is
nearly vertical; away from the critical point the error is an order of
magnitude smaller. The oxygen, methane and hydrogen tables reach from 0.1 to
60 MPa, so that they also cover the pumps (§5d). The reference set includes
ten turbopump states, from a pump inlet at a few bar on the cold liquid to
the 41 MPa of a hydrogen pump's discharge and the 48 MPa of an oxygen
preburner boost pump. At those states the tables agree with the equations of
state to 0.15 % or better. `Peng-Robinson agrees with the reference tables where a
cubic can` cross-checks the tables with a completely different EOS family and
confirms the tables are not systematically displaced.

**Sources:** methane — Setzmann & Wagner, *J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data* **20**
(1991) 1061; hydrogen — Leachman, Jacobsen, Penoncello & Lemmon, *J. Phys.
Chem. Ref. Data* **38** (2009) 721; oxygen — Schmidt & Wagner, *Fluid Phase
Equilibria* **19** (1985) 175; n-dodecane, the RP-1 surrogate — Lemmon &
Huber, *Energy & Fuels* **18** (2004) 960. Transport from the correlations
shipped with CoolProp for those fluids.

## 5b. Gas-side heat transfer against measurements

Every comparison above is against another *code*. This one is against
*experiments*, and it is the only place in this repository where an Ignis
prediction meets measured hardware data. There are two of them: air through a
cooled laboratory nozzle, where Ignis computes the comparison end to end, and a
hydrogen-oxygen rocket, where the published reduction is directly comparable
with the constant Ignis uses. They are independent, and they agree.

### Air through a cooled nozzle

**Source.** L. H. Back, P. F. Massier and H. L. Gier, *Convective Heat Transfer
in a Convergent-Divergent Nozzle*, JPL Technical Report No. 32-415 (1965);
NASA NTRS accession 19650010083. Air heated by the internal combustion of
methanol is expanded through a conical nozzle (throat diameter 1.803 in,
throat curvature radius 1.800 in, 30° convergent and 15° divergent
half-angles) and local wall heat flux is measured with thermocouple plugs.
The report defines `h = q_w/(T_aw − T_w)` with a Pr^(1/3) recovery factor,
which is the definition Ignis uses.

The tables were transcribed by hand from the scanned page images into
`validation/reference/nozzle_heat_transfer_reference.csv` and
`nozzle_contour_reference.csv`. The OCR of that scan is unreliable and was not
used; stations whose digits could not be read with certainty were dropped
rather than guessed, as were the stations the report flags as separated. The
transcribed contour was checked independently by rebuilding it from the stated
cone angles and throat arc, which reproduces the tabulated area ratios to
within 0.36 % from the throat downstream.

**The authors' own uncertainty** is about ±8 % on h in the throat at the
higher stagnation pressures and temperatures, rising to as much as ±21 % near
the inlet at low stagnation pressure, and they note these are maxima.

**Result.** Ratio of the Bartz coefficient Ignis computes to the measured one,
over 59 stations in four tests, using Ignis's own thermodynamics and transport
for air at the stagnation condition:

| Test | p<sub>t</sub>, psia | cooled approach | n | median | mean | range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 273 | 201.0 | 18 in | 19 | 1.44 | 1.47 | 1.14 – 2.15 |
| 271 | 175.0 | 18 in | 14 | 1.59 | 1.58 | 1.20 – 2.02 |
| 318 | 51.0 | none | 15 | 2.49 | 2.58 | 0.85 – 4.44 |
| 319 | 35.9 | none | 11 | 2.66 | 2.95 | 0.76 – 5.08 |

**Bartz over-predicts everywhere, and by much more on the 0-in. group.** The
median over-prediction is about 45 % with the 18-in. cooled approach and about
150 % with none. That it over-predicts at all is not an implementation fault:
the report's own Fig. 15 shows its curve for this same equation (their Ref. 22,
Bartz's "Simple Equation") lying above the data in the same way.

**What that split does *not* establish is the cause.** It is tempting to read
it as the inlet boundary layer — the 18-in. approach supplies the developed
turbulent layer the correlation assumes and the 0-in. cases do not — but the
four tests do not support that reading, because the two groups differ in more
than one thing at once:

| test | approach | p<sub>t</sub>, psia | Re<sub>D\*</sub> | K | K / K<sub>crit</sub> | Bartz/measured |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 319 | none | 35.9 | 4.6 × 10⁵ | 3.4 × 10⁻⁶ | 1.14 | 2.66 |
| 318 | none | 51.0 | 6.6 × 10⁵ | 2.4 × 10⁻⁶ | 0.80 | 2.49 |
| 271 | 18 in | 175.0 | 2.3 × 10⁶ | 7.0 × 10⁻⁷ | 0.23 | 1.59 |
| 273 | 18 in | 201.0 | 2.6 × 10⁶ | 6.1 × 10⁻⁷ | 0.20 | 1.44 |

Every 0-in. test is a low-pressure test and every 18-in. test is a
high-pressure one. Approach length, throat Reynolds number and the
acceleration parameter *K* = (ν/u²)(du/dx) therefore all track the error at
|r| > 0.99 — and all track each other at 0.98. With four points and that much
collinearity the dataset cannot attribute the error to any one of them. The
ranges asserted in the test are the observation; the explanation is not
claimed.

Both tables here are reproduced by `tools/analysis/throat_acceleration.py` and
`tools/analysis/jpl_confounding.py`.

(*K* above is computed from the closed-form throat gradient
4a\*/[(γ+1)<sup>1.5</sup>√(r<sub>t</sub>R<sub>c</sub>)], not a numerical
derivative — d*M*/d(*A*/*A*\*) is singular at *M* = 1, so a discrete gradient
there diverges under refinement. *K*<sub>crit</sub> ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶ is the usual
relaminarization threshold, Moretti & Kays 1965.)

`tests/validation/test_heat_transfer_validation.cpp` asserts these ranges. The
bounds record a measurement; they are not a target.

### A hydrogen-oxygen rocket

The obvious objection to the above is that it is *air*. Combustion products at
several thousand kelvin are a different fluid, and an over-prediction measured
in a 830 K laboratory nozzle need not carry across that gap. So the same
question was put to a rocket.

**Source.** E. J. Schacht, R. J. Quentmeyer and W. L. Jones, *Experimental
Investigation of Hot-Gas Side Heat-Transfer Rates for a Hydrogen-Oxygen
Rocket*, NASA Technical Note D-2832, Lewis Research Center (1965); NASA NTRS
accession 19650016811. A copper heat-sink thrust chamber — 5 in throat
diameter, 4.64 contraction and expansion area ratio, L\* = 54 in, 234-element
coaxial injector, 26 000 lbf at 900 psia — was fired on liquid oxygen and
gaseous hydrogen at chamber pressures from 157 to 966 psia and mixture ratios
from roughly 4.9 to 7.9. Transient wall temperatures at five axial stations
gave the gas-side flux. Transcribed to
`validation/reference/rocket_heat_transfer_reference.csv` from the scanned page
images, by hand, for the same reason as above.

**Why this one is a direct comparison.** The authors reduce every run to

```
St* Pr*^0.7 = C Re*_d^-0.2
```

and fit the constant *C* station by station. They then state that the *C* =
0.026 line "is similar to the simplified Bartz equation (ref. 12)" — which is
the correlation Ignis implements, with the same leading constant. So the
comparison is between their fitted *C* and the 0.026 sitting in
`include/ignis/thermal/HeatTransfer.hpp`, with no thermodynamic work in between
that could go wrong.

| Station | A/A\* | region | measured *C* | σ | 0.026 / *C* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.64 | chamber | 0.0257 | 0.0028 | **1.01** |
| 2 | 1.78 | convergent | 0.0240 | 0.0039 | 1.08 |
| 3 | 1.00 | throat | 0.0148 | 0.0020 | 1.76 |
| 3 (mean of 3, 3A, 3B) | 1.00 | throat | 0.0151 | 0.0020 | **1.72** |
| 4 | 1.27 | divergent | 0.0153 | 0.0016 | 1.70 |
| 5 | 3.33 | divergent | 0.0188 | 0.0015 | 1.38 |

Two things follow from that table.

**In the chamber, Bartz's constant is the measured constant.** 0.026 against
0.0257 ± 0.0028 is a 1 % difference, comfortably inside one standard
deviation. Where the gas is slow and the pressure gradient is mild, the
correlation is not approximately right — it is right.

**The over-prediction is concentrated at the throat**, which is precisely where
the favourable pressure gradient is strongest and the boundary layer is
furthest from the equilibrium turbulent profile the correlation assumes. The
factor there is 1.72, and the report puts it in its own words: the throat value
"is 42 percent lower than the widely used value of C = 0.026". It also
re-reduces four other investigations the same way and gets throat constants of
0.019, 0.017, 0.023 and about 0.018 — every one of them below 0.026.

**And that is the factor the air data already gave.** The JPL tests with the
18-in. approach over-predict by a median of 1.45; this rocket over-predicts at
the throat by 1.72. Different fluids, a gas several times hotter, a throat
nearly three times larger, different laboratories and different measurement
techniques — and they agree to within 16 %. The test asserts that agreement,
because two independent experiments landing on the same number is the part of
this section hardest to get by accident.

### What the two datasets together say about the cause

The JPL tests on their own cannot say (above). The rocket adds the constraint
that settles it, because it varies chamber pressure by a factor of six at
fixed geometry:

* **It is not primarily a Reynolds-number or relaminarization effect.** Across
  p<sub>c</sub> = 157 to 966 psia the throat *K* runs from 3.5 × 10⁻⁶ down to
  5.7 × 10⁻⁷ — from just above the relaminarization threshold to well below
  it — yet the report fits a single constant per station holding to 13 %. If
  the deficit were set by *K* crossing a threshold, or by any Reynolds
  dependence beyond the Re<sup>−0.2</sup> already in the fit, that constant
  would drift with pressure and it does not.
* **It is positional.** Read the station table again as a function of place
  rather than condition: 1.01 in the chamber, 1.08 in the convergent section,
  1.72 at the throat, 1.70 just downstream, 1.38 by A/A\* = 3.33. The error
  appears where the contraction has just distorted the boundary layer and
  fades as the layer re-equilibrates downstream.

That points at the structural assumption rather than at any one flow
parameter. Bartz is an *algebraic function of the local state*: it replaces the
boundary layer's momentum and enthalpy thickness — quantities that depend on
the whole path from the injector face — with a nozzle-scale length
D<sub>t</sub><sup>−0.2</sup> and a local mass-flux term (A<sub>t</sub>/A)<sup>0.9</sup>.
In the chamber, where the layer is a slow, pipe-like, fully-developed one, that
substitution is exact enough to reproduce the measured constant to 1 %. Through
the throat, where the layer has just been thinned and strained and is nowhere
near equilibrium with its local freestream, it is not. The correlation has no
memory, and the throat is where memory matters.

A note against false comfort: the Ignis-M1's own throat *K* is 4.7 × 10⁻⁷ to
9.3 × 10⁻⁷ depending on which curvature radius is used — below the
relaminarization threshold. That does **not** exempt it. The rocket at 966 psia
sits at 5.7 × 10⁻⁷, equally below threshold, and is still over-predicted by
1.72.

### The integral boundary layer against the same two experiments

Everything above measures Bartz. The model that replaced it as Ignis's default
— the integral turbulent boundary layer of [`theory.md` §12.1](theory.md),
marched from where the layer starts — was put against the same data. Its
closure (1/7-law profiles, Eckert reference state, Blasius skin friction,
Colburn analogy on the energy thickness, a start at Preston's minimum turbulent
Re<sub>θ</sub> = 320) was fixed from the textbook before either comparison was
run and has not been adjusted since. Both comparisons run through
`tests/validation/test_heat_transfer_validation.cpp`.

**The air nozzle.** The test article is rebuilt from the report's stated
geometry (2.53 in inlet, 30° cone, 1.800 in throat arc, 15° cone) rather than
interpolated between taps, because the area–Mach relation needs a smooth throat.
That rebuild matches the divergent taps to 0.7 %, but sits 2–4 % low in
A/A\* along the convergent cone (5.8 % at the first tap) — as though the real
cone were about 0.05 in further upstream; through h ∝ G<sup>0.8</sup> that is a
2–5 % low bias on the convergent stations, recorded rather than tuned away. The
layer starts where the cooled wall starts (18 in upstream for tests 271 and
273, at the nozzle inlet for 318 and 319); the report's measured inlet profiles
were *not* used, so this is a prediction and not a fit. Elliott, Bartz & Silver
(JPL TR 32-387) matched their starting thickness to the nozzle-entrance probe
for these very data; that is not done here.

| Test | p<sub>t</sub>, psia | cooled approach | boundary layer, median | Bartz, median | max *K* in the march |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 273 | 201.0 | 18 in | **0.94** | 1.44 | 2.8 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 271 | 175.0 | 18 in | **1.07** | 1.59 | 3.2 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 318 | 51.0 | none | 2.33 | 2.49 | 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ |
| 319 | 35.9 | none | 2.64 | 2.66 | 1.6 × 10⁻⁵ |

On the two high-pressure tests the boundary layer lands on the data — a median
of 1.00 over both, against Bartz's 1.45 — inside the report's ±8 % in the throat
region. On the two low-pressure tests it is as wrong as Bartz, 2.46 against
2.56. It says why: the acceleration parameter in its own march passes the
3 × 10⁻⁶ at which a turbulent layer starts to laminarise, by a factor of four
to five, and the run carries that warning. A turbulent closure cannot represent
a layer that is relaminarising, and this one does not try. (The *K* here is the
largest anywhere in the march, which for these tests is at the convergent
inlet, where the gas is slow; it is larger than the throat-only *K* in the
table further up.)

**The rocket.** TN D-2832's engine is rebuilt from its stated dimensions (5 in
throat, 4.64 contraction and expansion, 30° in and 15° out, 14.5 in from
injector to throat, which the rebuild reproduces to 1 %) and run through
Ignis's whole hot-gas path — equilibrium chamber, quasi-1D expansion, the layer
marched from the injector face, and the cooling module's gas-side survey at a
fixed wall temperature, since the hardware was a heat-sink nozzle. The station
positions are the report's own (−8.774, −2.125, 0, 1.5 and 8.026 in from the
throat, added to the reference file from the same page image). *C* is formed
the report's way, St\* Pr\*<sup>0.7</sup> Re\*<sub>d</sub><sup>0.2</sup> with
every property — density included — at the reference state and local static
pressure. Absolute *C* depends on the property set (the report says its own
choice moved *C* by 30 %), so the comparison is of shape: *C* at each station
over *C* at station 1.

| Station | region | measured | boundary layer | Bartz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | convergent | 0.93 | 0.83 | 1 |
| 3 (mean) | throat | 0.59 | 0.78 | 1 |
| 4 | divergent | 0.60 | 0.75 | 1 |
| 5 | divergent | 0.73 | 0.71 | 1 |
| rms error | | | **0.134** | 0.320 |

Bartz, a single constant, cannot fall at the throat; the boundary layer falls
by about half of what was measured, and its shape error is under half of
Bartz's. The result does not depend on the assumed wall temperature (0.134 at
500 K, 0.135 at 900 K). Two shortcomings are recorded, and asserted, rather
than averaged away: the throat dip is too shallow by about two standard
deviations of the measured ratio, and the measured *C* recovers downstream of
the throat ("high in the chamber, low in the throat, and increasing again at
the exit", in this engine and the three others the report re-reduced) where
the boundary layer's falls monotonically — so it matches station 5 for the
wrong reason. Laminarisation through the throat would deepen the dip; a cause
for the recovery is not established here.

**A caution from the source.** Elliott, Bartz & Silver, comparing this family of
methods with rocket motors, found the fluxes "usually above, rather than below"
the convective prediction and attributed it to secondary flows from injection,
mixing and combustion, which can put any mean-flow prediction "in error by as
much as a factor of two". The air data above has none of those; the rocket
here happens to sit below the prediction. Neither experiment is a reason to
believe the boundary layer is accurate to better than tens of percent in an
arbitrary engine.

### What this changes for the Ignis-M1

Switching the M1 from Bartz to the boundary layer moved its thermal picture in
the direction both experiments point — and moved its hot spot:

| | Bartz, no film (until now) | boundary layer, no film | boundary layer + 3 % fuel film (shipped) |
|---|---|---|---|
| peak gas-side wall temperature | 797.8 K at the throat | 996.2 K at the injector face | 692.3 K, 70 mm downstream |
| peak heat flux | 49.7 MW/m² | 42.1 MW/m² | 42.0 MW/m² |
| heat load | 14.67 MW | 11.99 MW | 11.27 MW |
| coolant temperature rise | 381.7 K | 300.8 K | 277.9 K |
| coolant pressure drop | 3.31 MPa | 2.10 MPa | 2.05 MPa |

At the throat the layer gives about 0.8 of Bartz's coefficient — consistent
with both measurements — and the heat load falls by a fifth. At the injector
face it gives about twice Bartz's: the turbulent layer starts there, thin, and
a thin layer conducts. Without a film the first few centimetres of the M1's
jacket ran near 1000 K, 200 K over the CuCrZr limit. That is the case real
chambers avoid with a film from the injector's outer row, which is also what
Wang & Luong (1994) credit for the low flux measured near the SSME's
faceplate, and the M1 now does the same: 3 % of its fuel as a wall film
([`theory.md` §12.4](theory.md)), which holds the whole jacket below 700 K at a
bracketed cost of 0 – 0.65 % in vacuum specific impulse. That is a design
change to a conceptual engine, made because the better model showed the
previous design to be infeasible at its injector end, and it is recorded as
such.

**The Monte Carlo dispersion.** `cooling.hot_gas_multiplier` (formerly
`bartz_multiplier`) is still dispersed lognormally about 1.0 with
σ<sub>ln</sub> = 0.15. Against Bartz that was shown above to be too narrow —
the two measurements implied multipliers 2.5 σ and 3.6 σ out. Against the
boundary layer the high-pressure air medians (0.94, 1.07) and the rocket's
shape error (13 %) sit inside it; the low-pressure air tests (relaminarising,
flagged) and the factor-of-two caution above do not. It is kept, as a
judgement, not a measurement.

## 5c. Film cooling against its own data

**Source.** J. E. Hatch and S. S. Papell, *Use of a Theoretical Flow Model to
Correlate Data for Film Cooling or Heating an Adiabatic Wall by Tangential
Injection of Gases of Different Fluid Properties*, NASA TN D-130 (1959); NTRS
19890068390. The correlation Ignis uses for wall films (its eq. 12) was fitted
to helium and air films in an 8 in square duct of heated air, and the report
prints the helium data (Table I). The 1/8 in and 1/2 in slot runs — 27 runs of
twelve wall temperatures each — were transcribed by hand from 300 dpi renders
of the page into `validation/reference/film_cooling_reference.csv`.

**Method.** For every point, the effectiveness is re-derived from eq. 12 with
the gas-side coefficient the report specifies (0.0265 (k/D<sub>h</sub>)
Re<sup>0.8</sup> Pr<sup>0.3</sup> at the mean of gas and coolant temperatures,
Ignis's own air properties, the duct pressure from continuity), the helium
density the authors' tabulated velocity implies, and helium's conductivity from
Petersen (Risø Report 224, 1970). The predicted wall temperature is compared
with the measured one.

**Result.** Inside the velocity-ratio range the report fitted (V<sub>g</sub>/V<sub>c</sub>
= 0.45 – 33.3) and at measured effectiveness ≥ 0.2, the range its ±5 % claim
covers: 257 points, mean |error| in wall temperature **4.0 %**, median +1.4 %,
65 % of them inside ±5 %. That is the report's claim, reproduced — it checks
the transcription of the equations and the reading of their inputs. Below the
range, in three 1/8 in runs where the helium jet is 2.3 – 3.8 times faster than
the air, eq. (11) is extrapolated, its velocity function reaches 300, and the
predicted wall runs 40 % hot on average (36 points). Ignis therefore holds the
ratio at the nearer end of the fitted range and warns.

**What it does not establish.** These are films of a few hundred kelvin of
air and helium in a gas stream below 1100 K, with no reaction. A rocket film —
dense supercritical fuel beside a 3500 K burning gas — is outside every one of
those ranges. The comparison shows the correlation is implemented as published,
not that it predicts a rocket film.

## 5d. Turbopumps against the RS-25's published data

`tests/validation/test_cycle_validation.cpp`. The *SSME Orientation* training
manual gives a "Key Performance Parameters" table for each of the RS-25's
four turbopumps at 104.5 % of rated power. Each table lists flow, inlet and
discharge pressure and efficiency for the pump, and flow, inlet temperature,
pressure ratio, efficiency and horsepower for the turbine. Every turbopump is
checked twice, independently.

**From the pump side.** Flow, pressures and efficiency give the power the
pump absorbs, which in steady running is the turbine's horsepower less the
bearing and seal losses the manual does not tabulate. The pumps follow the
real-fluid tables of §3. The manual gives pressures, not inlet temperatures:
the tank liquids are taken at their normal boiling points (LH<sub>2</sub>
20.3 K, LOX 90.2 K), and each high-pressure pump is fed at the low-pressure
pump's computed outlet temperature.

| Turbopump | Ignis pump-side power | Published turbine power | Ignis − published |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-pressure fuel (155 lb/s, 250 → 5,950 psia, η 75.0 %) | 61,599 hp | 63,080 hp | −2.3 % |
| High-pressure oxidiser: main pump (1,122 lb/s, 380 → 4,045 psia, η 71.8 %) + preburner pump (111 lb/s, 3,910 → 6,970 psia, η 75.8 %) | 22,394 hp | 22,880 hp | −2.1 % |
| Low-pressure fuel (155 lb/s, 30 → 290 psia, η 71.3 %) | 3,304 hp | 3,330 hp | −0.8 % |
| Low-pressure oxidiser (935 lb/s, 100 → 417 psia, η 67.7 %) | 1,605 hp | 1,614 hp | −0.5 % |

All four sit below the turbine figure, as bearing and seal losses would put
them. Taking the hydrogen as incompressible at its inlet density instead puts
the high-pressure fuel pump at 69,632 hp, 13 % above the real-fluid
integration and 10 % above the published turbine power. Its density rises
17 % between 1.7 and 41 MPa.

**From the turbine side.** Turbine flow, inlet temperature, pressure ratio and
efficiency give the power the turbine delivers. The two high-pressure turbines
run on fuel-rich preburner gas whose mixture ratio comes from the manual's
own flows. "76 percent of the hydrogen flow ... [goes to] two preburners ...
the fuel preburner receiving, by far, the larger share (50 percent versus 26
percent)" of the 155 lb/s, and each turbine passes its preburner's whole
output. That gives O/F (145 − 77.5)/77.5 = 0.871 for the fuel preburner and
(62 − 40.3)/40.3 = 0.538 for the oxidiser preburner. The low-pressure fuel
turbine is an expander turbine inside the staged-combustion engine: it runs on
the 29 lb/s of hydrogen that has cooled the main chamber, along the real-fluid
table.

| Turbine | Gas | Ignis | Published | Ignis − published |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-pressure fuel (145 lb/s, 1,330 °F, PR 1.50, η 81.1 %) | equilibrium H<sub>2</sub>/H<sub>2</sub>O at O/F 0.871 | 60,260 hp | 63,080 hp | −4.5 % |
| High-pressure oxidiser (62 lb/s, 870 °F, PR 1.53, η 74.6 %) | equilibrium H<sub>2</sub>/H<sub>2</sub>O at O/F 0.538 | 22,347 hp | 22,880 hp | −2.3 % |
| Low-pressure fuel (29 lb/s, +17 °F, 4,217 psia, PR 1.30, η 58.0 %) | real-fluid hydrogen | 3,327 hp | 3,330 hp | −0.1 % |

The high-pressure fuel turbine's −4.5 % is about what Ignis's ideal-gas
expansion would be expected to cost at 33 MPa. For pure hydrogen at that
state, CoolProp puts the real isentropic work over a 1.50 pressure ratio 5.6 %
above the ideal gas's (Z = 1.064). Ignis does not carry that correction
([`limitations.md` §4](limitations.md)).

The low-pressure fuel turbine's own page gives an inlet temperature of
**−17 °F** and no inlet pressure. The manual's flow schematic (p. 19) labels
the same 29 lb/s at the turbine inlet "+17 °F, 4,217 psia". The chamber's
coolant, which is what drives this turbine, leaves at +17 °F (p. 45). Ignis
uses the schematic's state and reads the page's −17 °F as a sign slip.
Between the chamber and the turbine the hydrogen is throttled from 4,441 to
4,217 psia, and hydrogen above its ~200 K inversion temperature warms when
throttled, so it cannot arrive 19 K colder than it left. At −17 °F the
turbine would come out about 6 % low. At 4,217 psia hydrogen is 22 % off the
ideal gas, which the real-fluid table carries.

**The preburners' own energy balance.** The flow schematic also gives each
preburner's inlet streams. The fuel preburner takes 77 lb/s of hydrogen at
−193 °F and 5,310 psia and 67 lb/s of oxygen at −252 °F and 5,636 psia, and
delivers gas at 1,310 °F. The oxidiser preburner takes 42 lb/s of the same
hydrogen and 25 lb/s of oxygen at 5,734 psia, and delivers gas at 871 °F.
Ignis solves the mixture ratio that burns at each published outlet
temperature, with each propellant carrying the enthalpy it has gained since
the tank, read from the real-fluid tables. The hydrogen has gained
1.94 MJ/kg. The published flows are the check:

| Preburner | Ignis O/F | O/F from the published flows | Ignis − published |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fuel (out at 1,310 °F) | 0.868 | 67/77 = 0.870 | −0.2 % |
| Oxidiser (out at 871 °F) | 0.612 | 25/42 = 0.595 | +2.9 % |

The 5 % tolerance is what a 20 K uncertainty in the outlet temperature means
for the mixture ratio (about 500 K per unit O/F here).

The tolerances (6 % on the high-pressure pumps, 15 % on the low-pressure
pumps, 8 % on the turbines, 5 % on the preburner mixture ratios) were set
before each comparison was first run, and all nine fall inside them.

**One finding from getting it wrong first.** The test's first version solved
each preburner's mixture ratio from its published temperature, with both
propellants at their tank temperatures. It got O/F 1.02 and 0.75, and turbine
powers 11 % and 14 % low. The real preburner hydrogen arrives warm, at −193 °F
rather than −423 °F: 27.5 % of the engine's hydrogen has passed through the
1,080 nozzle coolant tubes, and the rest runs through uninsulated ducts. Warm
hydrogen needs less oxygen to reach the same temperature, and the gas then
carries more hydrogen and more work per kilogram. With that warmth counted,
the energy balance gives the published mixture ratios (the table above).
Ignis's cycle closures therefore carry the pump work and the jacket's heat
into the generator or preburner propellants
([theory §14.2](theory.md#142-pumps-turbines-generators)).

**What this does and does not establish.** The components a cycle balance is
built from reproduce one engine's published turbopump powers to 0.1–4.5 %,
given that engine's own flows, pressures, temperatures and efficiencies. They
are real-fluid pumps, a frozen-equilibrium gas turbine, a real-fluid expander
turbine, and the preburner energy balance that sets the gas. It does not establish that Ignis's *closures* reproduce a
whole engine's balance. The RS-25's split fuel paths, two preburners, two
shafts and boost turbopumps are not what the single-turbine closure
represents, and no closed cycle has been compared with an engine.

**Source:** *Space Shuttle Main Engine Orientation*, Space Transportation
System Training Data, BC98-04, Boeing Rocketdyne Propulsion & Power, June
1998, pages as printed:
- "Propellant Flow Analysis", pp. 18–21;
- the Block IIA propellant flow schematic at 104.5 % RPL, p. 19;
- the main combustion chamber's Block IIA operating parameters, p. 45;
- the Block IIA Key Performance Parameters of the LPOTP (p. 53), LPFTP
  (p. 57), HPOTP (p. 59) and HPFTP (p. 67).

## 6. What is *not* validated

Stated plainly, because internal consistency is not accuracy:

* **No comparison against a measured *engine*.** §5b compares one correlation
  against two experiments, which is not the same thing. Ignis-M1 and Ignis-H1
  are conceptual engines invented for this project. Their predicted thrust,
  I<sub>sp</sub>, wall temperatures and coolant pressure drop have never been
  compared with a test stand, and no claim of real-engine accuracy is made
  anywhere in this repository. Neither §5b experiment has a regeneratively
  cooled wall, so the coupled gas/wall/coolant solve is unvalidated end to end;
  neither measures delivered I<sub>sp</sub>.
* **The thermal and cooling model is an engineering estimate.** The default
  hot-gas model, the integral boundary layer, lands on the high-pressure air
  data and halves Bartz's shape error against the rocket (§5b), but it
  relaminarisation-fails exactly as Bartz does on the low-pressure air tests,
  its throat dip in the rocket is too shallow, and the source authors warn that
  injection and combustion can put any mean-flow convective prediction off by
  up to a factor of two in a real engine. The film correlation is verified
  against its own 1959 helium data (§5c), far from rocket conditions. The Monte
  Carlo campaign disperses the hot-gas coefficient (`cooling.hot_gas_multiplier`,
  lognormal σ<sub>ln</sub> = 0.15). The 1-D fin/wall treatment ignores axial
  conduction, channel curvature effects and thermal stress.
* **The turbopump cycle closures are not validated against an engine.** Their
  components reproduce the RS-25's turbopump powers (§5d), but the gas-generator,
  staged-combustion and expander balances have only been verified for
  internal consistency (`tests/unit/test_cycle.cpp`). Pump and turbine
  efficiencies are inputs, and the shipped engines' values are assumptions.
  Fuel-rich methane and kerosene generator gas is taken as equilibrium, which
  it is not.
* **η<sub>c\*</sub> is an assumed number, not a prediction.** Ignis has no
  injector, mixing or combustion-length model. Every output reports the ideal
  and the corrected value separately so the assumption is always visible.
* **Separation prediction is empirical.** The Summerfield and Schmucker
  criteria are correlations of test data, and they disagree with each other by
  a noticeable margin; both are available and neither is presented as truth.
* **The transient is valid from ignition onward.** It solves a products-based
  0-D chamber and cannot represent the cold pre-ignition fill, ignition
  overpressure, or combustion instability.

See [`limitations.md`](limitations.md) for the complete list.
